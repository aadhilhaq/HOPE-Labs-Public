The target and hotspots

What the binder is designed against, which face of it, and the two things about this tool's numbering that will catch you out.

The target

Choose a structure opens the structure browser, the same dialog on every page of ours. Down its left are the places it offers to start: This computer, which opens your own file chooser and sends the file up; My scratch and Home; this tool's own run folder; the other tools' run folders, so a structure another tool made is two presses away; the group folders you can read; and a PDB code box with Fetch. Down its right is one folder at a time, with where you are, a way up, the folders, and then the structure files with their sizes. A code can also be typed straight into the card, and a file dropped anywhere on the card is the same as one chosen on This computer.

Once something is chosen, the card shows what was read off it: the file, its chains as chips with how many residues each holds and where its numbering runs, and the start of the sequence. Press a chip to leave that chain out of the target. Everything left out is dropped rather than kept out of the way, so a structure that arrived with its own bound peptide should have that chain left out, and the site can still be read off it with the button under the chain.

A .cif is taken as well as a .pdb. It is written as a PDB first, which is the copy the run is given and the one the chains, the ruler and the contig here are read off; 1YCR read in as an mmCIF comes out with chain A numbered 25 to 109, as it is in the entry, and the same 22-residue interface. Where it cannot be written as a PDB the page refuses it at the box and says why, rather than queueing a run to find out.

Under the chains, the page says anything it read that this tool's own reader cannot see: a nucleic acid strand, a ligand in a chain of its own, or a residue it has no name for. None of those is a chain to design against, and all of them are things the site can be read off.

The file is converted to an annotated mmCIF before anything else happens, because a condition spec will not read a PDB at all. The tool's own refusal says why: a PDB does not reliably carry the bonds and formal charges the model consumes. The conversion is the model's own, so missing atoms are completed and selenomethionine becomes methionine here rather than halfway through a run.

The site

The residues the binder has to reach. Press them on the chain the page draws under the target, or type them into the box as A63,A67,A73 or 63,66-70 when they are all on the first chain named; the chain and the box are one site, so each follows the other. They are sent as the C_HOT condition, which marks them as the residues the design must contact.

The chain is drawn in the structure's own numbering, ten residues to a group with the number above each. A residue the structure has not got, or one that falls in a gap in the density, is still refused against the box: the ruler can only offer the residues that are there, and the box will take anything typed into it.

A residue drawn without a letter is one this page's own reader has no residue for, such as a hetero group or an ion the file carries under the chain's name, and the number is still the one a hotspot is written in. Where a structure uses insertion codes, 21, 21A and 21B being three residues and one number, every residue is drawn and only the first of each number can be pressed: a selection names a residue by its number and has nowhere to put the code. Thrombin's heavy chain has twenty-eight of them.

Taking the site from a bound partner

Most targets worth designing against arrive bound to something. 1YCR is MDM2 with the p53 peptide in its cleft, and the residues that peptide touches are the site a binder wants. Leave the partner's chain out of the target and the page offers one button that marks every kept residue within 4.5 Å of it, saying how many it marked and from which chain. The partner need not be protein and need not be a chain this tool's own reader can see: a DNA strand, a ligand or a sugar in a chain of its own is a partner, because a contact is measured on atoms.

4.5 Å is the distance every page here counts a contact at, so a site taken from a partner and an interface read off a complex are the same residues by the same rule. On 1YCR it marks 22 residues of chain A. Prune them if you want a tighter site: a design asked to reach 22 residues at once is being asked for a larger binder than one aimed at seven.

A structure of three chains asks the other question: there may be no chain left out at all, and its chains still touch each other. Site from all chains marks every residue of the chains you are keeping that is within 4.5 Å of any other chain in the file, the ones you are designing against included. That is the interface a binder has to compete with, and on a trimer kept whole it is the only reading there is.

The viewer beside the chain draws the target with the site marked and the partner you left out faintly behind it. Pressing a residue there marks it too, which is the only way to mark a face you are looking at rather than one you have counted along a sequence.

The model also takes hotspots at the atom level, naming particular side-chain atoms. This page sends whole residues, because that is what a target card carries. For binder design that is the normal case.

No hotspots is allowed and is not the same as a mistake. With none named, the condition is left out and the binder may grow anywhere on the target. The run says so in its log rather than refusing.

A hotspot carrying an insertion code, H60A, is refused against the box. There is nowhere in a selection to put the letter: residues are named by number, and a structure with 60 and 60A in one chain cannot be converted at all, because both residues end up under one number and their atoms collide. Crop or renumber the target first.

The numbering, which is yours going in and not coming out

Going in, the numbers are the ones your structure carries. A residue is named in the contig and in every selection by the number it has in the file, so MDM2 in 1YCR is A25-109, not A1-85. This was measured against the tool rather than assumed: a file whose chain runs 17, 18, 30, 31 comes back out of the conversion as exactly those numbers, and a hotspot asked for as 30 lands on the residue that was 30.

That is worth saying plainly because the tool's own documentation warns about the opposite case. It names label_seq_id rather than author numbering, and for a structure deposited as mmCIF the two often differ. For anything this page converts from a PDB they are the same, and the preview shows you the result either way.

Show the job, under the run plan beside Submit, shows the contig and each condition, as the job will be given them:

contig: 70-100, /0, A25-109
C_CRDGRP: the target's backbone in one group, so it is held rigid with its side chains left free
C_SEQ: the target's own sequence
C_HOT: A63, A67, A73, by the numbers this structure carries

Read it before you submit. The range it took off your structure and every residue it will aim at are both in there.

Coming out, the design is renumbered. Each output chain is numbered from 1, so a motif no longer sits at the indices it went in at. What a run leaves says what that means for reading the output.

A target with gaps in its chain

Most crystal structures have holes in them. Residues nobody could see in the density are not in the file, and the numbering steps over them. The target is given to the tool as it was deposited, so the holes come with it.

Each chain goes in as one piece, with its stretches of resolved residues listed one after another and a chain break only between different chains:

contig: 70-100, /0, A1-190, A195-225, A228-270, /0, B1-85

This is the one place where this tool allows less than RFdiffusion3 does, and it is worth knowing rather than discovering. RFdiffusion3 can say "same chain, and no bond across this hole" by putting a chain break between two stretches of one chain. RFD4-Proteina cannot: a break starts a new output chain, and a contig whose two runs both name chain A is refused outright with Contig produces two separate output chains both named 'A'. So the stretches are listed in one run, and the model is told the chain runs straight through. The preview says how many gaps your target has, so you know when it applies.

A hotspot that falls in a hole is refused by name before anything is queued, because there is no such residue to aim at. The chips above the box say how each chain is numbered but not where its holes are, so a refusal naming the residue is how you find out.

What the target is told about itself

Two conditions are always set on the chains you named, and they are the authors' own recommendation for a protein against a protein.

C_CRDGRP puts every backbone atom of the target into a single coordinate group. That retains their geometry relative to one another, which is what holding a target rigid means, while leaving its side chains free to move. The alternative, C_CRD, fixes the atoms absolutely and reveals the side chains with them, and the authors warn that it produces binders that resemble the target.

C_SEQ reveals the target's own sequence, so the model knows what it is designing against rather than only the shape of it.

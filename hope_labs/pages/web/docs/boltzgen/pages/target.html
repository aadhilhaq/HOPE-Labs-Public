The target and hotspots

The target is what the binder is designed against, and the hotspots are the residues it should touch. The hotspots are also the one thing on this page that is not what you type, so the page shows you what they become.

Three ways to give a target

Where it comes fromWhat to do
PDB idFour characters. The entry is fetched from the RCSB on the login node, which has the internet, and copied into the run folder. A compute node has none, so a job cannot fetch its own target.
Structure fileDrop a .pdb or .cif file on the page or choose one. It is kept in the server's own scratch folder and copied into the run folder when you submit. An mmCIF is written as a PDB first, which is the file the run is given, and the positions in the chain below are counted off that copy.
Path on the clusterA path to a file already on Grace, for one too large to upload or one another tool left.
The positions are always counted off a PDB. The step that turns your residue numbers into positions in the chain reads the target by PDB column positions, so an mmCIF is written as one first and counted off that copy. Where it cannot be written as one the page says so at the box rather than letting a job discover it. The file's name is not what decides this: a PDB sent under a .cif name is read as the PDB it is, and an mmCIF sent as .pdb is converted, each with a line under the target saying which happened.

Chains

After the target is read, one chip appears per amino-acid chain, carrying the chain's letter, how many residues it has, and the first and last residue numbers it uses. Every chain is selected to begin with; click a chip to drop one.

The chains you keep are the ones written into the specification as the target. A chain that is dropped is not in the run at all.

Hotspots, in the structure's numbering

Write them the way every other tool in the lab takes them, which is the numbering the chips show:

What you typeWhat it means
54,56,66-70residues 54, 56 and 66 to 70 of the first chain you kept
A54,B12-16residue 54 of chain A and residues 12 to 16 of chain B
A54,56,5754, 56 and 57 of chain A: a residue with no letter takes the chain from the token before it
H60Arefused, and named. The conversion reads a chain, a number and at most a range, so an insertion code has nowhere to go: 60A would reach BoltzGen as the position of residue 60, which is a different residue, and one a structure numbered this way has as well. RFdiffusion3 keeps the code on its residue, so use that page when 60A is the site

A residue you name that the chain has not got turns the box red and names it. A run is not queued without hotspots.

Pressing the site onto the chain

Under the target the page draws each chain you are keeping in the structure's own numbering, ten residues to a group with the number above each, every residue a button. The box and the chain are one site, so typing in one marks the other. A number typed into a box cannot be checked by eye against the structure it names, and that is the mistake on this page that never announces itself: a site aimed at the wrong face designs for twelve hours and fails nothing.

A residue drawn without a letter is one this page's own reader has no residue for; the number is still the one a hotspot is written in. Where a structure uses insertion codes, so that 21, 21A and 21B are three residues and one number, every residue is drawn and only the first of each number can be pressed: a hotspot is a number and has nowhere to put the code.

Taking the site from a bound partner

Most targets worth designing against arrive bound to something. 1YCR is MDM2 with the p53 peptide in its cleft, and the residues that peptide touches are the site a binder wants. Leave the partner's chain out of the target and the page offers Site from chain B, which marks every kept residue within 4.5 Å of it and says how many it marked and from where. On 1YCR that is 22 residues of chain A. Prune them if you want a tighter site: a design asked to reach 22 residues at once is being asked for a larger binder than one aimed at seven.

4.5 Å is the distance every page here counts a contact at, so a site taken from a partner and an interface read off a complex are the same residues by the same rule. The partner need not be protein and need not be a chain this tool's own reader can see: a DNA strand, a ligand or a sugar in a chain of its own is a partner, because a contact is measured on atoms. The page names any such chain under the target.

Site from all chains asks the other question, the one a structure of three chains kept whole asks: not what the chain you left out touches but where these chains touch each other at all. It marks every residue of the chains you keep within 4.5 Å of any other chain in the file, the ones you are designing against included. On a trimer kept whole it is the only reading there is.

What the job is told

BoltzGen's specification numbers binding residues by their position in the chain, counting from 1 at the first residue present. That is not the numbering in the file. Under the hotspots box the page shows the conversion, one line per chain:

chain A: 54,56,66,67,68,69,70 in the file are positions 37,39,49,50,51,52,53
         (the chain is numbered 18 to 290)
the binder is asked for as "70..100"

Here the chain begins at residue 18, so residue 54 in the file is the 37th residue present. Both sets of numbers are shown because that is the only way to see the conversion is right. If the chain happens to start at 1 the two will match, and that is also correct.

The job checks it a second time before anything expensive starts. It asks BoltzGen to write the specification out as a structure with the binding residues marked, reads those marks back, and refuses to go on if they are not the positions that were asked for. A run that reaches the generation step has had its hotspots proved.

Gaps in a chain shift the positions. A crystal structure with a disordered loop missing has fewer residues present than its numbering suggests, and the conversion counts what is present. That is what BoltzGen wants, and it is another reason to read the line rather than assume it.

Choosing hotspots well

Three to six exposed residues on one face is the usual choice. A residue buried in the core cannot be bound and asking for it wastes the run; a set spread over two faces asks for a binder that cannot exist.

Coldspots

Coldspots are the opposite of hotspots, not the absence of them. BoltzGen has a not-binding type beside its binding one, so a residue named here is one the binder is asked to keep off, while a residue named in neither box is one the model is told nothing about at all.

They are typed the same way as hotspots, in the structure's own numbering, and they go through the same checks: a residue the target has not got is refused by name, and an insertion code is refused for the same reason it is in the hotspots box. They are converted to positions in the chain in the same line of the log.

What they are for is a second site on the same face. A target with a known interface beside the one you want is the case: naming it as a coldspot asks for a binder that reaches one and not the other, which is a harder question than naming only the hotspots and hoping.

A residue cannot be both. Given in both boxes it is refused, because what the binder is meant to do with it has not been said.

The target and hotspots

The target is the receptor the binder is designed against, and the hotspots are the residues it should touch. Both are read off the structure you give, so the page can tell you at once whether a residue you have named is a residue that exists.

Three ways to give a target

Where it comes fromWhat to do
PDB idFour characters. The entry is fetched from the RCSB on the login node, which has the internet, and copied into the run folder. A compute node has no internet, so a job cannot fetch its own target.
Structure fileDrop a .pdb file on the page or choose one. It is uploaded, kept in the server's own scratch folder, and copied into the run folder when you submit.
Path on the clusterA path to a file already on Grace. Use this for a file too large to upload, or for a structure a previous run or another tool left.
It has to be a PDB file. The job reads the target by PDB column positions, so an mmCIF is copied in and then found to contain no chains. The page refuses one rather than letting a job discover it twenty minutes in.

Whichever way you give it, the structure is read once and that one copy is what goes into the run. The RCSB answering twice is two files, and a target you have looked at should be the target that is designed against.

Chains

After the target is read, one chip appears per amino-acid chain, carrying the chain's letter, how many residues it has, and the first and last residue numbers it uses. Every chain is selected to begin with. Click a chip to drop that chain, and click it again to put it back.

A chain that is dropped is not in the run at all. It is not kept as context and it is not written into the design: RFdiffusion is given only the chains you keep, so a receptor whose second chain matters should keep both.

Hotspots

Hotspots are the residues on the target that the binder should make contact with. Write them in the numbering the chips show, which is the structure's own numbering:

What you typeWhat it means
54,56,66-70residues 54, 56 and 66 to 70 of the first chain you kept
A54,B12-16residue 54 of chain A and residues 12 to 16 of chain B
A54,56,5754, 56 and 57 of chain A: a residue with no letter takes the chain from the token before it
H60Arefused, and named. RFdiffusion keys a hotspot by its chain and its number alone and has nowhere to put an insertion code, so 60A would reach it as residue 60, which is a different residue, and one a structure numbered this way has as well. RFdiffusion3 keeps the code on its residue, so use that page when 60A is the site

A residue you name that the structure has not got turns the box red and names it, so a numbering that starts at 18 rather than 1 is caught while you are typing rather than hours later. A run is not queued without hotspots. Both of the lab's diffusion tools design far better with them, and a binder with nowhere in particular to go will find the largest hydrophobic patch on the surface rather than the site you care about.

What the job is told

Under the hotspots box the page shows the two lines RFdiffusion will actually be given:

contigmap.contigs=[A1-150/0 70-100]
ppi.hotspot_res=[A54,A56,A66,A67,A68,A69,A70]

The contig map says: keep chain A residues 1 to 150 as they are, break the chain so the binder is not fused to the target, then diffuse a new chain whose length is sampled between 70 and 100. The hotspot list is every residue spelled out, because RFdiffusion names them one at a time and has no range form of its own. Seeing these before you submit is the point of the box: a range written the wrong way round, or a chain you did not mean to keep, shows up in them.

A target with gaps in its chain

Most crystal structures have holes in them. Residues nobody could see in the density are not in the file at all, and the numbering steps over them: 7A3R's chain A is numbered 1 to 395 but has no 191 to 194, no 226 or 227, and three more holes besides. The target is given to the tool as it was deposited, so the holes come with it.

A span in the contig map means every residue number in it, and RFdiffusion checks that each one is really in the file, so a chain written 1 to 395 stops the run on the first residue it cannot find. The contig therefore gives each stretch of resolved residues a span of its own, divided by a slash, with the one chain break at the end:

contigmap.contigs=[A1-190/A195-225/A228-270/A275-342/A345-381/A386-395/0 70-100]

Inside one chain the divider is a plain slash and not /0 . RFdiffusion works the real distance between the stretches out of the numbering, so the model reads them as one chain with residues missing. A /0 would put a two hundred residue jump between them and tell the model that one protein is six separate ones. Two different chains do take /0 between them, because they really are separate molecules.

A hotspot that falls in a hole is refused by name before anything is queued, because there is no such residue to aim at.

Choosing hotspots well

Three to six residues on one face is the usual choice, and they should be residues that are actually exposed in the structure. A hotspot buried in the core cannot be touched, and asking for it wastes the run. If you know the epitope from a complex, take the residues the partner contacts; if you do not, a patch that is conserved and solvent exposed is a better guess than the whole face.

Naming too many residues is its own problem: RFdiffusion treats them as a set to make contact with, and a set spread over two faces asks for a binder that cannot exist.

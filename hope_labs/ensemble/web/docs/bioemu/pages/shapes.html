The shapes, and what they are not

What the groups of samples mean, how they are found, and the four things an ensemble cannot tell you however much it looks as though it could.

An ensemble is not a trajectory

The samples are independent draws from the distribution BioEmu learned. They are not steps along a path, and the order they come out in means nothing at all. Four things follow, and all four matter because an ensemble in a viewer looks exactly like a trajectory:

  • No rates and no barriers. Nothing here says how fast a protein moves between two shapes, or what it has to climb to get there. Those are kinetics and they are what a simulation is for.
  • No pathway. Two shapes in one ensemble are two shapes the chain takes. The way it gets from one to the other is not in the samples and cannot be read off them.
  • Nothing to play. The viewer steps through the shapes because that is how a viewer shows several structures. What you are seeing is a slideshow, not a film, and it should never be presented as one.
  • No time axis anywhere. Nothing on this page or in its report is plotted against sample number, deliberately. A drift along the sample axis would be read as motion in time, and it would mean nothing.

What an ensemble does carry is how often: the share of the samples in each shape is an estimate of how much of its time the protein spends there, which is exactly what a long simulation is usually run to find out.

How the shapes are found

Every sample is aligned onto every other and the distance between them measured as the root mean square deviation of the alpha carbons. The samples are then grouped by complete linkage and the grouping cut at 0.3 nm.

Three choices there, each for a reason:

Alpha carbons only, because the model writes a backbone and one side chain atom per residue, so a heavy atom deviation would be a backbone deviation with a different weighting and would not be comparable with the numbers a simulation of the same protein is quoted by.

0.3 nm, because below it two structures differ by detail this model does not claim to resolve, and above it they are different enough to be worth showing side by side. It is the figure the ensemble literature uses for the same fold in a different arrangement.

Complete linkage, because the question is whether every sample in a group is the same shape as every other. Single linkage answers a different question and will happily chain two distinct shapes together through whatever lies between them.

The representative of a shape

The sample closest to all the others in its own group: a real structure the model drew, not an average. Averaging a spread of shapes gives something flatter than any of them, with bond lengths nothing could have, which is why the mean shape is used here only as the point distances are measured from and never shown.

Reading the numbers

One shape, narrow spread. The model is confident the chain holds one arrangement. Worth checking that the shape is the one you expect.

One shape, wide spread. A fold that is held but loose, or a chain with a flexible tail. The per residue fluctuations say which it is: a tail shows as a few residues moving far while the rest hold still.

Two or three shapes with real populations. The interesting answer. Open them side by side and look at what differs. This is the result that is invisible in any single structure, and it is the reason to run this at all.

Many shapes, each small. Usually a chain the model has no confident answer for: a long disordered region, or a sequence far from anything it was trained on. Treat the populations as noise, and look at the length and at what the training range was.

What would settle it

An experiment. Failing that, a simulation of the same chain, which is a different kind of evidence rather than a better one: against a simulation.

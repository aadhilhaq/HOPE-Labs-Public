Overview

BioEmu samples the structures a protein actually takes, from its sequence, in minutes. This page runs it on Grace, reads the samples for you and puts the answer beside a simulation of the same chain.

What this answers

A folding tool answers one question: what shape is this sequence. It gives one structure, or a few that differ in the parts it is least sure about, and a confidence for each.

That is not the question most protein work turns on. A protein is not one shape. It holds a fold and moves within it, opens and closes, frays at a loop, and sometimes sits in two arrangements with a population each. BioEmu answers what shapes does this sequence take, and how often, by sampling from a distribution it learned from simulation and from measured folding free energies.

The usual way to ask that question is molecular dynamics, which integrates the physics for days. BioEmu answers it in minutes. It does not replace a simulation, and the shapes, and what they are not is the page that says exactly what it does and does not give you.

What a run does here

You paste one protein sequence and say how many structures to draw. The run then has two phases, because a compute node on this cluster reaches no network at all:

  1. Here, on the login node. The alignment for your sequence is searched for on the ColabFold server. BioEmu makes its embeddings from an alignment, and this is the only part of a run that needs the internet.
  2. On the GPU queue. The sampling, inside the tool's own container image, with the weights that were fetched when it was installed, and with nothing reaching out anywhere. The samples are then read in the same job.

The two phases explains why that split exists and what it means when something fails.

What you get back

The samples as one trajectory file with a topology beside it, which is how every tool that reads ensembles expects them, and on top of that what reading them found: how wide the spread is, how many distinct shapes are in it, what share of the samples each shape holds, which residues move and which do not, and one structure per shape that you can open in the viewer or put in a slide. What a run leaves lists the files.

Against a simulation

The reason this page sits beside HOPE-MD rather than beside the folding pages is that you can point it at a HOPE-MD run of the same chain and ask whether the two agree: whether the simulation visited the shapes that were sampled, whether the two say the same residues move, and whether their spreads overlap. Minutes against days, on the same protein. Against a simulation is that page.
